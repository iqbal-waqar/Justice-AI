fed-01 What kind of suit can be filed under Section 42 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877?

Justice AI's answer

A suit under Section 42 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 is a suit for a declaratory decree, where a person entitled to any legal character or right to property may sue against anyone denying or interested to deny that title or right. The court may, at its discretion, declare the plaintiff entitled to such character or right, and the plaintiff need not seek any further relief in that suit. However, if the plaintiff is able to seek further relief beyond a mere declaration and omits to do so, the court shall not grant such a declaration (Section 42, Specific Relief Act, 1877). Section 42 suits are specifically for the declaration of pre-existing rights or legal status, not for creating new rights. The right to sue accrues when there is either an actual denial or an apprehended/threatened denial of the right. For example, adverse entries in revenue records may constitute a threatened denial, giving rise to a fresh cause of action, but actual ousting from possession constitutes an actual denial (Salamat Ali v. Muhammad Din, PLD 2022 SC 353; Muhammad Azram v. Muhammad Altaf, 2024 LHC 1120). The court’s discretion under Section 42 is to declare the status or right but not to grant further relief such as possession or damages unless the plaintiff seeks it separately. The declaration is binding only on the parties to the suit and those claiming through them (Specific Relief Act, 1877, Section 42 and 43). The Supreme Court in Muhammad Siddique v. Mst. Noor Bibi (2020 SCMR 483) emphasized that a Section 42 suit cannot create fresh rights; it only declares existing rights. Similarly, in Ahmad v. Manzoor Ahmad (2022 LHC 4369), the Lahore High Court held that a declaratory decree requires the plaintiff to establish a pre-existing right that is being denied, and vague or uncertain claims (such as oral agreements without particulars) cannot sustain such a suit. In summary, a suit under Section 42 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 is a declaratory suit to establish legal character or property rights where those rights are denied or threatened to be denied, and the court may declare the plaintiff’s entitlement without granting further relief unless sought. Practical points: - File a Section 42 suit when your legal character or property right is denied or threatened. - Ensure you plead and prove a pre-existing right or title. - If you want relief beyond declaration (e.g., possession), seek it explicitly. - Be mindful of limitation periods, especially if actual denial (e.g., ouster) occurs.

Section 42 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 allows a suit for a declaration: any person entitled to any legal character, or to any right to any property, may sue any person who denies, or is interested in denying, his title to that character or right, and the court may in its discretion declare that he is so entitled without the plaintiff asking for any further relief. However, the court shall not make such a declaration where the plaintiff, being able to seek further relief than a mere declaration of title, omits to do so. Such a suit is to be decided within six months and an appeal within ninety days. Under Section 43, the declaration binds only the parties and persons claiming through them.

CHAPTER VI
OF DECLARATORY DECREES
42. Discretion of Court as to declaration of status or right. Bar to such declaration.–
Any person entitled to any legal character, or to any right as to any property, may institute a
suit against any person denying, or interested to deny, his title to such character or right, and
the Court may in its discretion make therein a declaration that he is so entitled, and the
plaintiff need not in such suit ask for any further relief:
Provided that no Court shall make any such declaration where the plaintiff, being able to
seek further relief than a mere declaration of title, omits to do so.
Explanation.– A trustee of property is a “person interested to deny” a title adverse to the
title of some one who is not in existence, and for whom, if in existence, he would be a trustee.
Illustrations
(a) A is lawfully in possession of certain land. The inhabitants of a neighboring village
claim a right of way across the land. A may sue for a declaration that they are not
entitled to the right so claimed.
(b) A bequeaths his property to B, C and D, “to be equally divided amongst all and each of
them, if living at the time of my death, then amongst their surviving children”. No such
children are in existence. In a suit against A’s executor, the Court may declare whether B,
C and D took the property absolutely, or only for their lives, and it may also declare the
interests of the children before their rights are vested.
(c) A covenants that, if he should at any time be entitled to property exceeding one lakh of
rupees, he will settle it upon certain trusts. Before any such property accrues, or any persons
entitled under the trusts are ascertained, he institutes a suit to obtain a declaration that the
covenant is void for uncertainty. The Court may make the declaration.
(d) A alienates to B property in which A has merely a life interest. The alienation is invalid as
against C, who is entitled as reversioner. The Court may in a suit by C against A and B
declare that C is so entitled.
(e) The widow of a sonless Hindu alienates part of the property of which she is in possession
as such. The person presumptively entitled to possess the property if he survive her may,
in a suit against the alliance, obtain a declaration that the alienation was made without
legal necessity and was therefore void beyond the widow’s lifetime.
(f) A Hindu widow in possession of property adopts a son to her deceased husband. The
person presumptively entitled to possession of the property on her death without a son
may, in a suit against the adopted son, obtain a declaration that the adoption was
invalid.
(g) A is in possession of certain property. B, alleging that he is the owner of the property,
requires A to deliver it to him. A may obtain a declaration of his right to hold the
property.
(h) A bequeaths property to B for his life, with remainder to B’s wife and her children if
any, by B, but if B die without any wife or children, to C. B has a putative wife, D,
and children, but C denies that B and D were ever lawfully married. D and her
children, may, in B’s lifetime, institute a suit against C and obtain therein a
declaration that they are truly the wife and children of B.
43. Effect of declaration.– A declaration made under this Chapter is binding only on the parties
to the suit, persons claiming through them respectively, and, where any of the parties are trustees,
on the persons for whom, if in existence at the date of the declaration, such parties would be
trustees.

fed-02 On what grounds can a decree be challenged under Section 12(2) CPC?

Faithfulness 1.00Answer relevancy 0.96Context precision 0.64Context recall 1.00Factual correctness 0.50

Justice AI's answer

A decree can be challenged under Section 12(2) of the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC) primarily on the grounds of fraud, misrepresentation, or want of jurisdiction. This provision does not allow reopening the merits of the case but is limited to setting aside a decree obtained by fraudulent means or where the court lacked jurisdiction (Rubina Amjad v. Javaid Shafique Siddique, Islamabad High Court, 2022). The Lahore High Court in Muhammad Sharif v. Muhammad Ramzan (2023) emphasized that a decree alleged to have been obtained by fraud must be examined on merits rather than dismissed on technical grounds. The court held that such a suit should be treated as an application under Section 12(2) CPC, allowing the court to frame issues, record evidence, and decide the matter accordingly. In the context of arbitration awards made rule of the court, the Lahore High Court in Shamsa Hameed v. ASJ Khushab (2023) clarified that a decree based on an award obtained by collusion or fraud can be challenged under Section 12(2) CPC. The court distinguished between challenging the award itself (under the Arbitration Act) and challenging the decree passed on the basis of the award, which can be impeached under Section 12(2) CPC if fraud is alleged. However, an order passed under Section 12(2) CPC itself is not a decree because it lacks the formal expression of adjudication in a suit, as explained in Khalid Hussain v. PSIC Employees Cooperative Housing Society (2020). Therefore, such orders cannot be challenged as decrees but as orders. To summarize, the grounds for challenging a decree under Section 12(2) CPC are: - The decree was obtained by fraud or misrepresentation. - The decree was passed by a court lacking jurisdiction. - The challenge must not be on the merits of the case but on procedural or jurisdictional defects involving fraud. - Decrees based on arbitration awards obtained by fraud can also be challenged under this section. Practical steps: - File an application under Section 12(2) CPC specifically alleging fraud, misrepresentation, or lack of jurisdiction. - The court may frame issues and record evidence to determine the validity of the decree. - Do not attempt to challenge the merits of the original suit through Section 12(2) CPC. - Distinguish between challenging the arbitration award itself (under the Arbitration Act) and the decree based on that award (under Section 12(2) CPC).

Reference answer

Under Section 12(2) CPC, a person challenging the validity of a judgment, decree or order on the plea of fraud, misrepresentation or want of jurisdiction must apply to the court that passed the final judgment, decree or order, and not file a separate suit. The grounds are that the decree was passed without jurisdiction, was obtained by misrepresentation of facts, or was obtained by fraud. The scope of Section 12(2) is not limited to fraud; it includes misrepresentation, meaning a false statement of facts. A decree passed without jurisdiction is void and can be set aside under this section.

Justice AI's answer

Under the Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act 2016, a court can pass several types of orders to protect and provide relief to an aggrieved woman facing violence. These include interim orders, protection orders, residence orders, and monetary orders, each tailored to address different aspects of violence and its consequences. The Act empowers the court to issue **interim orders** at any stage of the complaint if it is prima facie satisfied that the defendant has committed or is likely to commit violence (Section 6). These interim orders are flexible and aimed at immediate protection. A **protection order** (Section 7) is issued if the court is satisfied that violence has occurred or is likely. The court may direct the defendant to: - Cease communication with the aggrieved person, - Stay away from her or maintain a specified distance, - Wear a GPS tracking bracelet in cases of grave violence, - Move out of the shared house if the aggrieved person's life or dignity is at risk, - Surrender weapons or be prohibited from acquiring firearms, - Refrain from aiding violence or entering places frequented by the aggrieved person, - Avoid causing violence to dependents or helpers of the aggrieved person, - Comply with any other conditions necessary for protection. The court may also require the defendant to execute a bond to prevent further violence and can direct Women Protection Officers and police to assist in enforcing these orders. In cases of **domestic violence**, the court may issue a **residence order** (Section 8) to: - Prevent eviction of the aggrieved person from the house without her consent, - Affirm her right to stay in the house, - Allow relocation to a shelter home for protection and rehabilitation, - Direct the defendant to hand over possession of property or documents to which the aggrieved person is entitled, - Restrain the defendant or relatives from entering the shelter home or places frequented by the aggrieved person, - Arrange or pay for alternative accommodation. The court considers the financial circumstances of the parties before passing orders with financial implications. Finally, the court may pass a **monetary order** (Section 9) directing the defendant to pay compensation or monetary relief to cover expenses and losses suffered by the aggrieved person due to violence. These orders are designed to provide comprehensive protection, relief, and rehabilitation to women subjected to violence, with the court empowered to tailor orders to the facts and circumstances of each case. - Interim orders to prevent imminent violence or harm. - Protection orders imposing restrictions on the defendant’s conduct and movements. - Residence orders securing the aggrieved person’s right to housing and safety. - Monetary orders for compensation and expenses related to violence. The court may also involve Women Protection Officers and police to ensure enforcement and protection under these orders.

Justice AI's answer

A landlord may seek eviction of a tenant under the Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009 primarily on grounds specified in Section 15 of the Act. These grounds include expiry of the tenancy period, failure of the tenant to pay rent within 30 days after the due date, breach of any term or condition of the tenancy agreement, and violation of tenant obligations under Section 13 of the Act. Section 15 of the Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009 explicitly lists the grounds for eviction as follows: - (a) Expiry of the tenancy period. - (b) Tenant’s failure to pay or tender rent within thirty days after the stipulated period. - (c) Breach of any term or condition of the tenancy agreement by the tenant. - (d) Violation of tenant obligations under Section 13, which include keeping the premises in proper condition, using the premises for the agreed purpose, allowing landlord inspection, handing over vacant possession on termination, not causing nuisance, and not making structural changes without landlord consent. The Lahore High Court in *Raja Zulfiqar Ali vs Muhammad Sadiq* (2024 LHC 3726) confirmed that expiry of the tenancy period and non-payment of rent after expiry are valid grounds for eviction under Section 15(a) and (b). Similarly, in *Mushtaq Ahmad vs Ishfaq Ahmed* (2022 LHC 2556), eviction was sought on grounds of default in rent payment and unauthorized structural changes, which are breaches under Section 13 and 15. The court in *Muhammad Mujahid Fareed vs Additional District Judge* (2018 LHC 3795) held that unauthorized structural changes by the tenant without landlord’s consent constitute sufficient ground for eviction under Section 13(1)(f) and Section 15(c). Further, the landlord must establish the relationship of landlord and tenant, including proof of rent payment, to maintain an eviction petition as emphasized in *Ch. Abdul Waheed vs Zahida Parveen* (2021 LHC 9616). Without proof of tenancy and rent payment, eviction under the Act cannot be granted. In practice, the landlord must: - Show expiry of tenancy or default in rent payment. - Prove breach of tenancy agreement or tenant obligations. - File an eviction application before the Rent Tribunal or Special Judge (Rent). - Provide evidence of tenancy relationship and rent payment. Thus, under the Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009, eviction is legally permissible when the tenancy period expires, rent is unpaid beyond 30 days, or the tenant breaches contractual or statutory obligations. - File eviction petition citing specific grounds under Section 15. - Provide tenancy agreement and rent payment evidence. - Seek leave to contest if tenant resists eviction. - Argue breach of tenant obligations if applicable (e.g., unauthorized structural changes).

Reference answer

Under Section 15 of the Punjab Rented Premises Act 2009, a landlord may seek eviction of a tenant if the period of tenancy has expired; the tenant has failed to pay or tender rent within thirty days after the period stipulated in Section 7; the tenant has breached a term or condition of the tenancy agreement; the tenant has violated an obligation under Section 13; the premises are used for a purpose different from that for which they were let; or the tenant has sub-let without the landlord's prior written consent. The eviction petition is filed before the Rent Tribunal, and the tenant cannot defend it unless he obtains leave to contest, applied for within ten days of first appearance with an affidavit; leave is refused unless the application discloses sufficient grounds for recording oral evidence. Courts have ordered eviction where the tenant defaulted in paying rent.

pun-03 What demands must a pre-emptor make under the Punjab Pre-emption Act 1991?

Justice AI's answer

Under the Punjab Pre-emption Act 1991, a pre-emptor must make demands of pre-emption in a strict sequential order to preserve and enforce the right of pre-emption. These demands are known as Talb-i-Muwathibat, Talb-i-Ishhad, and Talb-i-Khusumat, as prescribed by Section 13 of the Act. The first demand, **Talb-i-Muwathibat**, requires the pre-emptor to make an immediate oral declaration of his intention to exercise the right of pre-emption in the sitting or meeting (Majlis) where he first learns of the sale. This demand must be made promptly upon knowledge of the sale, and any words indicating the intention to pre-empt suffice (Haji Khizer Hayat v. Abid Hussain, 2020 LHC 311; Pre-emption Act, 1991, Section 13(1)(a)). The second demand, **Talb-i-Ishhad**, must be made as soon as possible but no later than two weeks from the date the pre-emptor gains knowledge of the sale. It involves sending a written notice to the vendee, attested by two truthful witnesses, under registered cover with acknowledgment due, confirming the pre-emptor’s intention to exercise the right. In areas lacking postal facilities, this notice may be made in the presence of two truthful witnesses (Pre-emption Act, 1991, Section 13(3); Muhammad Farooq Khan v. Muhammad Ashiq, 2020 LHC 1503; Bashir Ahmad v. Iqbal Ahmad, 2021 LHC 4185). The third demand, **Talb-i-Khusumat**, is the filing of a suit in a court of competent jurisdiction to enforce the right of pre-emption, but only after the first two demands have been properly made (Pre-emption Act, 1991, Section 13(4); Muhammad Sagheer v. Fazal Karim, 2022 YLR-N 23). The courts have emphasized that these Talbs are not mere formalities but mandatory conditions precedent for the enforcement of the right of pre-emption. Failure to comply with any of these steps, especially the timely and proper service of Talb-i-Ishhad, results in the extinguishment of the right (Muhammad Farooq Khan v. Muhammad Ashiq, 2020 LHC 1503; Muhammad Sagheer v. Fazal Karim, 2022 YLR-N 23; Bashir Ahmad v. Iqbal Ahmad, 2021 LHC 4185). In summary, the demands a pre-emptor must make under the Punjab Pre-emption Act 1991 are: - **Talb-i-Muwathibat:** Immediate oral declaration of intention to pre-empt upon knowledge of sale. - **Talb-i-Ishhad:** Written notice to vendee within two weeks, attested by two witnesses, sent by registered post or made in presence of witnesses if postal facilities are unavailable. - **Talb-i-Khusumat:** Filing a suit in court after fulfilling the first two demands. To protect the right of pre-emption, a pre-emptor must strictly follow this sequence and meet the procedural requirements for each demand.

Reference answer

Under Section 13 of the Punjab Pre-emption Act 1991, the right of pre-emption is extinguished unless the pre-emptor makes three demands in order: talb-i-muwathibat, talb-i-ishhad and talb-i-khusumat. Talb-i-muwathibat is the immediate demand, made in the same sitting (majlis) in which he learns of the sale, declaring his intention to pre-empt; any words showing that intention are enough. Talb-i-ishhad is the demand by establishing evidence: as soon as possible and not later than two weeks from the date of knowledge, he must send the vendee a written notice attested by two truthful witnesses by registered post acknowledgement due, or make it before two truthful witnesses where postal facilities are unavailable. Talb-i-khusumat is the demand by filing a suit. To prove talb-i-muwathibat the plaintiff must state the exact date, time and place at which he gained knowledge of the sale.

Reference answer

If the officer in charge of the police station refuses to register an FIR, the aggrieved person should first approach the police officers higher in rank than the SHO, and then may apply under Section 22-A(6) Cr.P.C. to the Ex-Officio Justice of Peace (a Sessions or Additional Sessions Judge), who can direct the police to record the statement of the complainant under Section 154 Cr.P.C. The Justice of Peace must satisfy himself that the higher police officers were approached first. His role is one of vigilance to redress refusal to register reports; he must not conduct a full investigation or act as a prosecutor before the FIR is registered.
