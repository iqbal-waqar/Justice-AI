crim-01 What are the grounds for pre-arrest bail under Section 498 Cr.P.C.?

Justice AI's answer

The grounds for pre-arrest bail under Section 498 Cr.P.C. are well established by the Supreme Court and Lahore High Court through authoritative judgments. Pre-arrest bail is an extraordinary remedy granted only in exceptional cases where the petitioner shows a good prima facie case, and it is demonstrated that arrest and refusal of bail would likely be motivated by mala fide intentions or ulterior motives to injure or humiliate the accused, causing irreparable harm (Rana Muhammad Arshad v. Muhammad Rafique, PLD 2009 SC 427; Ahtisham Ali v. The State, 2023 SCMR 975; Muhammad Rafie v. Ghaneem Aabir, 2024 LHC 938). The courts have consistently held that: - The petitioner must establish reasonable grounds to believe he is not guilty of the offence and that the case requires further inquiry (Section 497(2) Cr.P.C.). - There must be a genuine and imminent apprehension of arrest causing virtual restraint or custody. - The arrest must be shown to be sought for ulterior motives, such as harassment or abuse of process by the police, rather than to further the ends of justice. - The petitioner should physically surrender to the court and not be a fugitive or have a past criminal record that disentitles him to bail. - Pre-arrest bail is not a substitute for post-arrest bail and should be granted sparingly to avoid hampering investigation. - The High Court or Sessions Court has concurrent jurisdiction to grant pre-arrest bail, but the petitioner should ordinarily approach the court of first instance first. These principles were laid down in the Full Bench decision in Hidayat Ullah Khan v. The Crown (PLD 1949 Lahore 21), reaffirmed in Rana Abdul Khaliq v. The State (2019 SCMR 1129), and applied in subsequent cases such as Malik Tariq Hanif Awan v. State (2020 LHC 1794) and Muhammad Fayyaz v. State (2019 LHC 3198). In summary, the grounds for pre-arrest bail under Section 498 Cr.P.C. require: - A good prima facie case for bail on merits. - Proof of mala fide or ulterior motive behind the arrest. - Imminent or actual restraint or custody. - No disqualifying factors like fugitive status or past criminal record. - The petitioner must first seek relief from the Sessions Court unless justified otherwise. This framework ensures that pre-arrest bail protects innocent persons from victimization without undermining the criminal justice process.

Reference answer

Pre-arrest bail is an extraordinary relief, granted only in extraordinary situations to protect innocent persons from victimisation through abuse of law for ulterior motives. It is not a substitute for post-arrest bail. The accused must satisfy the conditions of Section 497(2) Cr.P.C., i.e. show reasonable grounds that he is not guilty or that the case calls for further inquiry, and must also show that the intended arrest is motivated by ulterior motives such as humiliating or injuring him, or that he would suffer irreparable harm if arrested. Section 498 Cr.P.C. is an independent provision, but bail under it can only be granted to a person who is present in court, and the stringent criteria do not stop the court from granting it where the record plainly shows false implication.

Section 489-F PPC specifically criminalizes the act of dishonestly issuing a cheque for repayment of a loan or fulfillment of an obligation that is dishonoured on presentation. The dishonesty element requires mens rea, meaning the cheque must be issued with the intention to cause wrongful loss or gain (Muhammad Anwar v. The State, 2019). Mere dishonour of a cheque is not sufficient; the prosecution must prove dishonesty and that the cheque was issued for repayment of a loan or obligation (N.R.S.P Microfinance Bank Ltd v. Justice AI's answer

A wife seeking khula under Pakistani law is generally required to return the dower (mahr) she received from her husband, but this obligation is not absolute and depends on the circumstances of the case and the court’s discretion. The return of dower is primarily linked to khula granted under Section 10(4) of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act, 1964, where the dissolution is granted immediately after failure of reconciliation without full trial. However, if khula is granted after evidence and trial, the court may decide on the return of dower based on the facts, including whether the wife was at fault or compelled to seek khula. The Lahore High Court in *Muhammad Kamran v. Mst. Sumera Majeed* (2018 LHC 3749) held that the proviso to Section 10(4) of the Family Courts Act requires the wife to restore the dower when khula is granted immediately after reconciliation fails. The court emphasized that this rule applies only to khula cases where the decree is passed forthwith without trial. If the case proceeds to trial, the court has discretion to order return of dower or not, depending on the circumstances, including fault of the parties. Similarly, the Lahore High Court in *Muhammad Mohsin Raza v. ADJ* (2022 LHC 7184) explained that if the wife seeks khula without fault on her part and is compelled by circumstances beyond her control, she may not be required to surrender the dower at all. Conversely, if the wife seeks khula without valid reason or fault of the husband, she must surrender the entire dower. The court has discretion to proportionately reduce the amount of dower to be returned if both parties are partly at fault. The Federal Shariat Court, as cited in *Asif Mehmood v. ADJ Sahiwal* (2025 LHC 2359), reinforced that the return of dower in khula cases is not automatic. If the wife seeks khula due to her dislike without fault on the husband’s part, she must return the dower. But if the wife proves ill-treatment or fault on the husband’s part, the court may reduce or waive the return of dower entirely. The Islamabad High Court in *Nasir Mehmood v. Learned ADJ Islamabad* (2023 CLC 350) also upheld that where khula is granted at the reconciliation stage without full trial, the wife must return the dower. However, if the case involves full evidence and trial, the court may exercise discretion. In summary, the legal position under Pakistani law is: - If khula is granted immediately after failure of reconciliation (no trial), the wife must return the dower (Section 10(4) proviso, Family Courts Act). - If khula is granted after trial and evidence, the court decides on return of dower based on fault and circumstances. - If the wife is at fault or seeks khula without valid reason, full return of dower is required. - If the wife is compelled by the husband's fault or ill-treatment, the court may reduce or waive the return of dower. Practical steps: - In khula petitions, argue the facts carefully to show whether the wife was at fault or compelled. - Present evidence of fault or ill-treatment to seek waiver or reduction of dower return. - If khula is granted at reconciliation stage, expect the court to order return of dower. - Courts have discretion; each case depends on its facts and evidence.

Reference answer

Generally, where a wife obtains khula she must return the dower and benefits received from the husband, or remit the dower if not yet received, and she cannot later claim the dower as of right. However, return of dower is not an indispensable condition for the grant of khula: once the Family Court finds the wife entitled to khula it must decree dissolution, and non-return of dower only creates a civil liability without affecting the validity of the decree. Where the wife seeks khula because of the husband's fault and gives reasonable justification, she should not be required to return the dower already received, and the court decides how much of the benefits should be returned in the circumstances.

Justice AI's answer

An agreement becomes a contract under Section 10 of the Contract Act, 1872, when it is made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration, with a lawful object, and is not expressly declared void by law. This means the parties must agree voluntarily, be legally capable of contracting, and the agreement must have a lawful purpose and consideration (Federal Government Employees Housing Authority v. Granite Enterprises (Pvt) Ltd, Islamabad High Court, 2025; Said Rasool v. Maqbool Ahmed, Supreme Court of Pakistan, 2023). The law defines an agreement as a promise or set of promises forming the consideration for each other, and a contract as an agreement enforceable by law (Contract Act, 1872, Sections 2(e), 2(h), and 10). The essential elements include mutual consent (consensus ad idem), competence of parties (age of majority and sound mind), lawful consideration, and lawful object. The consent must be free, not caused by coercion or fraud (Contract Act, 1872; Rao Abdul Rehman v. Muhammad Afzal, Supreme Court of Pakistan, 2023). The courts have held that contracts may be oral or written, and the absence of writing or signatures does not invalidate a contract unless a specific law requires it (Muhammad Sattar v. Tariq Javaid & others, Supreme Court of Pakistan, 2016). The intention to be legally bound can be inferred from the parties’ conduct and objective manifestations of consent (Said Rasool v. Maqbool Ahmed, 2023). However, where the law requires contracts to be in writing or witnessed, those formalities must be observed. Key provisions involved are: - Section 10: All agreements are contracts if made by free consent of competent parties, for lawful consideration and object, and not declared void. - Section 11: Competence to contract (majority, sound mind, no disqualification). - Section 13 and 14: Consent and free consent. - Sections 8 and 9: Acceptance and proposal can be express or implied. To establish a valid contract under Section 10: - Ensure parties are competent (majority and sound mind). - Confirm free consent without coercion or fraud. - Verify lawful consideration and lawful object. - Observe any statutory requirements for writing or registration if applicable. The bottom line is that an agreement meeting these criteria under Section 10 of the Contract Act, 1872, is a valid contract enforceable by law.

11. Who are competent to contract. Every person is competent to contract who is of the age

disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject.
12. What is a sound mind for the purposes of contracting. A person is said to be of sound
mind for the purpose of making a contract if, at the time when he makes it, he is capable of
understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests.
A person who is usually of unsound mind, but occasionally of sound mind, may make a
contract when he is of sound mind.
A person who is usually of sound mind, but occasionally of unsound mind, may not make a
contract when he is of unsound mind.
Illustrations
(a)

A patient in a lunatic asylum, who is at intervals of sound mind, may contract
during those intervals.

(b)

A sane man, who is delirious from fever or who is so drunk that he cannot
understand the terms of a contract or form a rational judgment as to its effect
on his interests, cannot contract whilst such delirium or drunkenness lasts.
